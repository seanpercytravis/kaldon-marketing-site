## Factory audit questions: what to ask on the call and on-site

Start with a live factory video call before you book travel or send an inspector. Request a walkthrough that shows production floor, QC stations, raw material storage, and packaging lines. Trading companies will refuse or show generic office space. Real factories will walk you through machinery, introduce floor managers, and show work-in-progress orders.

**Key questions for the video call:**

- Can you show me the production line for my product category right now?
- Who is the plant manager, and can I speak to them?
- How many lines are running today, and what are you producing?
- Can you show me your raw material inventory and QC inspection area?
- What is your current lead time, and how many orders are ahead of mine?

If they hesitate, reschedule, or only show a showroom, you are likely dealing with a trading company or broker.

**On-site audit questions (or third-party inspection checklist):**

- Verify the legal entity name on the factory entrance sign matches the business license and your contract.
- Count production lines and compare to claimed capacity. A factory claiming 10,000 units per month should have equipment and staff to support it.
- Check employee headcount. Walk the floor during shift hours. Empty factories during business hours signal subcontracting risk.
- Inspect raw material suppliers. Ask for purchase orders or supplier invoices. Verify they match your product spec.
- Review QC documentation. Real factories have inspection logs, defect rates, and corrective action records. Ask to see last month's reports.
- Confirm certifications on-site. If they claim ISO 9001 or BSCI, the certificate should be posted, current, and match the legal entity name. Photograph it and verify with the issuing body later.

For China, use a third-party inspection company like QIMA, SGS, or Intertek for the first order. Cost is $200 to $500 per inspection. For USA or Europe, the same firms operate domestically, or you can hire a local quality consultant.

## Alibaba red flags: what signals a trading company or scam

Alibaba's Gold Supplier and Verified badges are not proof of legitimacy. They confirm the company paid a fee and submitted documents Alibaba checked, not that they are a factory or safe to wire money to.

**Red flags that signal trading company or elevated risk:**

- Company name includes "Trading," "International," "Import/Export," or "Commerce" instead of "Manufacturing" or "Factory."
- Alibaba storefront shows products across unrelated categories (electronics, textiles, home goods). Real factories specialize.
- Office address in Shenzhen, Guangzhou, or Yiwu commercial district instead of an industrial zone. Cross-reference the address in Google Maps satellite view. Look for factory buildings, not office towers.
- Supplier refuses factory visit or video call, or only offers to meet at a showroom or trade fair booth.
- Business license entity name does not match the Alibaba store name or the name on the contract. This is a disqualifying red flag.
- Bank account name differs from business license name. In China, legitimate factories use company accounts, not personal accounts.
- Pressure to pay via Western Union, MoneyGram, or personal bank transfer instead of Trade Assurance or PayPal.
- MOQ (minimum order quantity) is unusually low for the claimed factory. A factory with injection molding tooling will not accept 100-unit orders.
- No response to technical questions about materials, tolerances, or production process. Trading companies cannot answer these without asking their factory.

**How to verify the company is real in China:**

1. Get the exact legal entity name (in Chinese characters) from the business license.
2. Search the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System at gsxt.gov.cn. This is the official Chinese corporate registry.
3. Confirm registration date, address, registered capital, and legal representative match the documents you received.
4. Check for litigation, penalties, or abnormal operating status. If the company is flagged, walk away.

For Alibaba specifically, use Trade Assurance for the first three orders minimum. Trade Assurance covers payment if the supplier fails to meet contract terms (shipping date, product quality per inspection). It is not perfect, but it adds a dispute layer between you and the wire transfer.

## Sample protocols: how to test quality before committing to bulk

Order samples before negotiating bulk terms. Samples verify the supplier can produce to spec and ship on time. They also expose quality fade, communication gaps, and logistical friction.

**Sample order checklist:**

- Request samples of your exact product, not generic catalog items. Specify materials, colors, dimensions, and packaging.
- Pay for samples. Free samples are often generic stock, not custom production. Paying $50 to $200 for samples filters out non-serious suppliers.
- Request 3 to 5 units. One sample can be an outlier. Multiple units expose consistency issues.
- Specify shipping method and timeline. If they miss a sample deadline by two weeks, expect worse on bulk.
- Inspect samples against your spec sheet. Measure dimensions, test materials, check stitching or welds, and compare to reference images.
- Test functionality and durability. If you are selling kitchenware, cook with it. If you are selling electronics, run it for 48 hours.
- Document defects with photos and notes. Send feedback to the supplier and see how they respond. Good suppliers ask clarifying questions and propose corrections. Bad suppliers defend defects or go silent.

If samples pass, request a **pre-production sample** from the actual bulk order before full payment. Pre-production samples use the final materials, tooling, and production line. They confirm the supplier did not switch specs between sample approval and bulk manufacturing.

**Red flags during sampling:**

- Supplier ships a different product than discussed, then claims it is "similar quality."
- Materials feel cheaper than the sample (thinner plastic, lower thread count, different metal alloy).
- Packaging is generic or missing your branding even though you specified custom packaging.
- Supplier refuses to send additional samples or charges exorbitant fees ($500+ for a second sample of a $10 product).

## Payment terms: protecting deposits and managing risk

Never pay 100% upfront, even if the supplier offers a discount. Standard terms for new suppliers are 30% deposit, 70% before shipment. Once you have a relationship and verified quality across 3+ orders, you can negotiate net terms or reduce the deposit.

**Payment structure for first order:**

- 30% deposit to start production (via Trade Assurance, PayPal, or escrow).
- 70% balance after production, before shipment, triggered by a final inspection report from your third-party inspector.
- Retain 5% to 10% as a quality holdback, released 30 days after delivery and your final QC check (only feasible with established suppliers, not first orders).

**Safe payment methods:**

- **Trade Assurance (Alibaba):** Covers order value if supplier fails to ship on time or meet quality terms. Disputes are adjudicated by Alibaba. Not foolproof, but better than a wire.
- **PayPal Goods and Services:** Offers buyer protection up to $20,000 per transaction. Supplier pays a 3% to 5% fee, so many resist. Use it for samples and first small orders.
- **Escrow services (Escrow.com, Payoneer Escrow):** Third party holds funds until you confirm receipt and quality. Adds 1% to 3% fee but eliminates wire risk.
- **Letter of Credit (LC):** Bank-backed payment released when supplier meets documented shipping and quality terms. Common for orders over $50,000. Requires setup with your bank and costs $500 to $2,000 in fees.

**Payment methods to avoid for new suppliers:**

- Western Union, MoneyGram, or direct bank transfer (T/T) to a personal account.
- Cryptocurrency (no recourse if the supplier disappears).
- Full payment before production starts, even with a "discount."

If a supplier insists on unsafe payment terms and refuses Trade Assurance or PayPal, find a different supplier. The 10% to 15% you save on price is not worth a 100% loss if they vanish.

## ISO, BSCI, and certifications: what to verify and how

Suppliers list certifications to signal quality and compliance, but certificates are easy to fake. Verify every certification directly with the issuing body before you rely on it.

**Common certifications and what they mean:**

- **ISO 9001 (Quality Management):** Factory has documented processes for quality control, corrective action, and continuous improvement. Does not guarantee product quality, only that a system exists.
- **ISO 14001 (Environmental Management):** Factory tracks environmental impact and waste. Relevant for sustainability claims.
- **BSCI (Business Social Compliance Initiative):** Factory passed a social audit for labor conditions (working hours, wages, safety, child labor). Required by many EU buyers.
- **FDA registration:** For food, cosmetics, or medical devices sold in the USA, the factory must be registered with FDA. Check the registry at accessdata.fda.gov.
- **GMP (Good Manufacturing Practice):** Required for food, supplements, cosmetics. Confirms sanitary production and traceability.
- **CE marking (Europe):** Product meets EU safety and environmental standards. CE is self-declared by the manufacturer, not third-party certified, so verify test reports.
- **UL, ETL, FCC (USA electronics):** Third-party safety and emissions testing. Verify certificate numbers at UL.com or FCC.gov.

**How to verify certifications:**

1. Request the full certificate as a PDF, not just a logo on the website.
2. Check the certificate number, issue date, expiry date, and scope. Certificates often cover specific product categories or processes, not the entire factory.
3. Verify the legal entity name on the certificate matches your supplier's business license and contract.
4. Contact the issuing body. For ISO, search the auditor (SGS, TUV, BSI) and call their verification hotline. For FDA, search the registry online. For UL, use the online certification directory.
5. Check for expired certificates. An expired ISO 9001 from 2019 is not evidence of current compliance.

If the supplier refuses to provide the full certificate or the certificate number does not verify, assume it is fake.

**Specific requirements for food and supplement importers (USA):**

FDA's Foreign Supplier Verification Program (FSVP) requires U.S. importers to document supplier verification for all foreign food manufacturers. This includes finished goods, ingredients, and private-label products. You must evaluate supplier risk, choose verification methods (on-site audits, HARPC documentation, testing), and re-verify at least every two years or more often for high-risk suppliers. FSVP compliance is a written program with quickly retrievable records to show due diligence during FDA inspections. If you skip this and a recall happens, you risk losing your import privileges and paying penalties. If you are importing food or supplements, treat FSVP as a required cost, not optional.

## ThomasNet vs Made-in-China vs Alibaba vs domestic sourcing: when to use each

The platform you use changes the verification approach because each has different supplier types and trust signals.

**Alibaba (alibaba.com):**

- **Best for:** China-based manufacturers, high-volume orders, products with existing tooling.
- **Supplier mix:** 60% trading companies, 40% factories. Verification is critical.
- **Trust signals:** Trade Assurance, Gold Supplier badge (weak), response rate, years on platform.
- **Verification approach:** Business license check via gsxt.gov.cn, factory video call, third-party inspection, Trade Assurance for payment.
- **Risk level:** High for new sellers, medium once you verify.

**Made-in-China (made-in-china.com):**

- **Best for:** Industrial components, machinery, B2B sourcing. Less retail-focused than Alibaba.
- **Supplier mix:** Similar to Alibaba, mix of factories and trading companies.
- **Trust signals:** Verified supplier badge (paid membership), transaction history.
- **Verification approach:** Same as Alibaba. Cross-check business license, video call, third-party inspection.
- **Risk level:** High. Treat verification the same as Alibaba.

**ThomasNet (thomasnet.com):**

- **Best for:** USA and Canada manufacturers, industrial sourcing, custom manufacturing, low-to-mid volume.
- **Supplier mix:** Predominantly real manufacturers. Trading companies are rare.
- **Trust signals:** Years in business, certifications, case studies, client references.
- **Verification approach:** Check state business registration (Secretary of State website), request client references and call them, visit facility if possible, verify certifications (ISO, ITAR, AS9100 for aerospace).
- **Risk level:** Low to medium. USA suppliers are easier to verify and have legal recourse, but you still need to confirm capacity and quality.

**Global Sources (globalsources.com):**

- **Best for:** Electronics, tech accessories, high-mix sourcing.
- **Supplier mix:** Mix of factories and trading companies, slightly more vetted than Alibaba.
- **Trust signals:** Verified supplier, trade show presence, export data.
- **Verification approach:** Same as Alibaba. Business license, video call, third-party inspection.
- **Risk level:** Medium to high.

**Domestic sourcing (USA, EU, UK):**

- **Best for:** Short lead times, small batches, products requiring close collaboration, Made in USA claims.
- **Supplier mix:** Mostly real manufacturers, some contract manufacturers and fulfillment centers.
- **Trust signals:** Business registration, BBB rating, client reviews, facility visit.
- **Verification approach:** Check state/country business registry, request and call references, visit facility or do virtual tour, verify insurance (product liability, workers comp), confirm capacity with production schedule.
- **Risk level:** Low. Legal recourse is easier, but cost is 2x to 5x China.

**When to choose domestic over China:**

- MOQ under 500 units (China factories often require 1,000+ units).
- Lead time under 4 weeks (China is 6 to 12 weeks plus shipping).
- Frequent design changes (easier communication and iteration).
- Made in USA is a core brand value or regulatory requirement.
- Product liability risk is high (supplements, baby products, electronics), and you want easier legal recourse.

**When to choose China:**

- MOQ over 1,000 units and you have verified demand.
- Cost is the primary constraint, and you can absorb 8 to 14 week lead times.
- Product category is commoditized (phone cases, kitchen tools, apparel).
- You have time and budget for proper verification (video calls, inspections, samples).

## What to document and keep on file

Documentation protects you during disputes, audits (FDA, FTC, customs), and product liability claims. Treat supplier verification as a compliance file, not a one-time checklist.

**Required documents for every supplier:**

- Business license (with English translation if not in English).
- Factory photos and video walkthrough with timestamp.
- Signed contract with legal entity name, scope of work, payment terms, lead times, and quality standards.
- Certifications (ISO, BSCI, GMP, etc.) with verification notes and expiry dates.
- Sample approval records: photos, spec sheets, your written approval.
- Third-party inspection reports (pre-production, during production, final inspection).
- Payment receipts and wire confirmations.
- Shipping documents (commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading).
- Communication log: key emails, WeChat/WhatsApp conversations about spec changes or delays.

**For FDA-regulated categories (food, supplements, cosmetics):**

- FSVP written program.
- Supplier risk evaluation (hazard analysis, risk rating).
- Verification method documentation (audit reports, HARPC records, lab test results).
- Re-verification schedule and completion records.

**For Made in USA claims:**

- Bill of materials with supplier origin for each component.
- Manufacturing location and process documentation.
- Cost breakdown showing domestic content percentage.
- Signed attestation from supplier confirming origin.

Store these files in a secure, organized system. Use a folder structure like Supplier Name > Year > Document Type. ## Common verification mistakes that cost sellers money

Most supplier failures are not scams. They are preventable errors where the seller skipped a verification step or misread a signal.

**Mistake 1: Trusting platform badges without independent verification.**

Alibaba Gold Supplier, Made-in-China Verified, and Amazon Brand Registry badges confirm the company paid a fee and submitted documents. They do not confirm the company is a factory, has the capacity you need, or will ship quality products. Always verify business license, factory video, and third-party inspection independent of platform trust signals.

**Mistake 2: Skipping the factory video call because you are in a hurry.**

A 30-minute video call exposes trading companies, brokers, and fake factories before you wire money. If you skip it to save time, you risk losing the entire deposit. Schedule the call during business hours in the supplier's time zone and request a live floor walkthrough. If they reschedule more than once or only show an office, walk away.

**Mistake 3: Paying 100% upfront for a "discount."**

Suppliers offer 5% to 15% discounts for full payment upfront. The discount is not worth the risk on a first order. Stick to 30% deposit, 70% before shipment. Once you have verified quality across 3+ orders, negotiate better terms.

**Mistake 4: Assuming samples match bulk production.**

Quality fade is common. Suppliers ship high-quality samples, then switch to cheaper materials or subcontract bulk production to a lower-tier factory. Always order a pre-production sample from the bulk order and run a third-party inspection before the final 70% payment.

**Mistake 5: Not verifying the bank account name matches the business license.**

In China, legitimate factories use company bank accounts. If the bank account is under a personal name or a different company, you are wiring money to the wrong entity, which voids Trade Assurance and makes disputes nearly impossible. Confirm the bank account name in writing before you send the deposit.

**Mistake 6: Skipping third-party inspection because the supplier "guarantees" quality.**

Supplier guarantees are not enforceable without evidence. A $300 third-party inspection catches defects before shipment and gives you documented proof for disputes. Skipping it to save $300 on a $10,000 order is false economy.

**Mistake 7: Using the supplier's "recommended" inspection company.**

Some suppliers offer to arrange inspection through their "partner" company. This is a conflict of interest. The inspector works for the supplier, not you. Hire your own third-party inspector directly (QIMA, SGS, Intertek, or a local firm).

**Mistake 8: Not reading the fine print on Trade Assurance or PayPal protection.**

Trade Assurance covers on-time shipping and product quality per your contract, but disputes require documented proof (inspection reports, photos, spec sheets). PayPal Goods and Services covers "item not received" and "item not as described," but you must file within 180 days and provide evidence. Read the terms before you assume you are fully protected.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I verify a supplier on Alibaba is a real factory and not a trading company?

Request a live factory video call showing production lines, QC stations, and raw material storage during business hours. Cross-check the business license entity name at gsxt.gov.cn (China's official registry) and confirm it matches the contract and bank account name. Trading companies refuse factory visits, show only offices, or list unrelated product categories. Use a third-party inspection (QIMA, SGS) for the first order and pay via Trade Assurance.

### What payment terms are safe for a first order with a new supplier?

Pay 30% deposit via Trade Assurance or PayPal Goods and Services to start production, then 70% balance after a third-party inspection confirms quality but before shipment. Never pay 100% upfront, even for a discount. Avoid Western Union, MoneyGram, cryptocurrency, or wire transfers to personal bank accounts. Once you verify quality across 3+ orders, negotiate net terms or lower deposits.

### How do I verify ISO or BSCI certifications are real and current?

Request the full certificate PDF (not just a logo) and check the certificate number, issue date, expiry date, and legal entity name matches your supplier's business license. Contact the issuing body directly (SGS, TUV, BSI for ISO; Amfori for BSCI) using their verification hotline or online directory. Expired certificates or mismatched names are disqualifying red flags.

### When should I use ThomasNet instead of Alibaba for sourcing?

Use ThomasNet for USA/Canada manufacturers when you need MOQs under 500 units, lead times under 4 weeks, frequent design changes, or Made in USA claims. ThomasNet suppliers are predominantly real manufacturers with easier verification (state business registration, client references, facility visits) and legal recourse. Use Alibaba for high-volume orders (1,000+ units), commoditized products, and cost-driven sourcing where you can absorb 8 to 14 week lead times and invest in verification (video calls, third-party inspections).

### What documents should I keep on file for supplier verification?

Store business license (with translation), factory photos/video with timestamps, signed contract, certifications with verification notes, sample approvals, third-party inspection reports, payment receipts, shipping documents, and communication logs. For FDA-regulated categories (food, supplements, cosmetics), also keep FSVP written program, supplier risk evaluation, and re-verification records. For Made in USA claims, document bill of materials with origin and manufacturing location for each component.
