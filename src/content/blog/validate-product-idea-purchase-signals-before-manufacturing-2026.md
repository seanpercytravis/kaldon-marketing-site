## Why keyword volume and trend spikes are not validation

Keyword volume tells you how many people searched. It does not tell you whether they bought, what they paid, whether they returned the product, or whether anyone can manufacture and ship it profitably.

Trend spikes indicate attention. They do not indicate purchasing intent, price tolerance, or margin.

Likes, video views, and survey responses are vanity metrics. They cost the user nothing. A person who clicks "interested" may never visit your landing page. A person who completes a survey may have no budget. A person who likes a TikTok may already own a substitute.

The recurring failure mode is reliance on vanity metrics: views, likes, trend spikes, or broad search volume without validating price, conversion, margins, returns, and supply reliability.

**What counts as a purchase signal:**

- Pre-order with payment (strongest)
- Deposit or partial payment
- Booked call or demo with calendar commitment
- Waitlist sign-up with specific product configuration
- Click from ad to landing page to checkout (even if abandoned)
- Signed pilot agreement or LOI
- Competitor sale data (actual transaction, not listing view)

**What does not count:**

- Survey interest without price anchor
- Likes, shares, or video views
- Comments saying "I need this" without click-through
- Keyword volume without conversion data
- AI tool recommendation without external evidence
- Prototype feedback from friends or colleagues with no budget

Sellers are being advised to separate four questions: Does the problem exist? Will people click or sign up? Will they pay? Can the product be manufactured and fulfilled profitably? Validation must answer all four before committing to inventory.

## The four-part validation framework: demand, margin, supply, operations

A complete validation framework tests four domains before manufacturing. Each domain has a riskiest assumption. Test that assumption first.

### 1. Demand validation: Will people pay the price you need?

**Riskiest assumption:** People want the product at a price that covers your costs plus margin.

**Cheapest test:** Landing page with price, call-to-action, and payment or waitlist form. Drive 100-500 clicks from paid ads targeting your exact customer. Measure click-to-signup or click-to-purchase rate.

**Evidence required:**

- 2-5% landing-page-to-signup conversion (waitlist, pre-order, or booked call)
- 0.5-2% landing-page-to-payment conversion if accepting money
- 10+ specific complaints, substitutes, or objections from comments or interviews
- 180-day stable or growing search demand (not a 14-day spike)

**Red flags:**

- Zero signups after 500 clicks
- High bounce rate (under 10 seconds on page)
- Comments asking "why not just use [substitute]?"
- Price sensitivity: users click but abandon at checkout

### 2. Margin validation: Can you sell it profitably?

**Riskiest assumption:** The product can be sold at a price customers will pay, after accounting for landed cost, platform fees, returns, and customer acquisition cost.

**Cheapest test:** Get written supplier quotes for three order quantities (100, 500, 1,000 units). Add shipping, duties, platform fees, payment processing, returns reserve, and CAC. Calculate margin at each tier.

**Evidence required:**

- Landed cost per unit (product + shipping + duties + packaging)
- Platform fees (Amazon referral, FBA, Shopify transaction, Walmart commission)
- CAC from test ads (total ad spend ÷ conversions)
- Return rate assumption (5-15% for most categories)
- Break-even price and target margin (30%+ for most eCommerce)

**Red flags:**

- Margin under 20% at 1,000-unit scale
- CAC higher than first-order gross profit
- Returns or customer service cost not modeled
- Supplier quotes that expire in 30 days or require 50% deposit without samples

### 3. Supply validation: Can the product be manufactured on workable terms?

**Riskiest assumption:** A supplier can produce the exact variant, on schedule, at the quoted cost, with acceptable quality and payment terms.

**Cheapest test:** Order samples from 2-3 suppliers. Inspect for defects, packaging, branding compliance, and shipping time. Request written quotes with MOQ, lead time, payment terms, and revision policy.

**Evidence required:**

- Sample matches spec (size, material, color, branding)
- Written quote with MOQ under 500 units for first order
- Lead time under 45 days for production + shipping
- Payment terms allow 30-50% deposit, balance on delivery or inspection
- Supplier has verifiable trade history (Alibaba transactions, references)

**Red flags:**

- No samples available, or samples ship from different location than production
- MOQ over 1,000 units with no negotiation
- 100% payment required before production
- No revision or defect policy
- Supplier cannot provide proof of prior production for similar item

### 4. Operations validation: Can you fulfill, support, and scale it?

**Riskiest assumption:** You can handle fulfillment, returns, customer service, and compliance at the volume required to hit profitability.

**Cheapest test:** Map the operational workflow. Identify fulfillment options (FBA, 3PL, self-fulfillment). Estimate return and support volume. Check compliance requirements (FDA, FTC, CPSC, platform policies).

**Evidence required:**

- Fulfillment cost per unit (picking, packing, shipping, storage)
- Return and refund policy compliant with platform rules
- Customer support plan (email, chat, phone) with estimated hours per 100 orders
- Compliance checklist (product safety, labeling, restricted categories)
- Inventory management plan (reorder point, lead time buffer)

**Red flags:**

- No plan for returns or defective units
- Fulfillment cost exceeds 15% of sale price
- Product requires FDA approval or restricted-category certification without timeline
- No reorder plan (one-time buy with no supplier relationship)

## How to rank evidence from weakest to strongest

Validation evidence forms a hierarchy. Higher tiers are harder to obtain but carry more weight in a go/no-go decision.

**Tier 1: Opinions and interest signals (weakest)**

- Survey responses without price
- Likes, shares, video views
- Comments saying "I need this"
- Friends or colleagues saying "I would buy that"

**Tier 2: Observed behavior (weak)**

- Keyword search volume (no conversion data)
- Competitor listing views (not sales)
- Social media engagement (comments, saves)
- Landing page visits (no signup)

**Tier 3: Low-commitment intent (moderate)**

- Waitlist signup with email
- Landing page click-through from paid ad
- Product quiz or configuration tool completion
- Cart add (no purchase)

**Tier 4: High-commitment intent (strong)**

- Booked call or demo with calendar hold
- Checkout abandonment with email capture
- Specific feature request with contact info
- Pre-order with no payment (name + email + product config)

**Tier 5: Purchase commitment (strongest)**

- Deposit or partial payment
- Full pre-order payment
- Signed pilot or LOI
- Completed purchase (even if refunded later)

A defensible validation decision requires evidence in Tier 4 or Tier 5 before committing to manufacturing. Tier 1-3 evidence can guide creative, messaging, or feature prioritization, but it cannot substitute for purchasing behavior.

## How to build a fact base when signals conflict

Real-world validation often produces conflicting signals. High search volume but low conversion. Strong waitlist signups but high cart abandonment. Positive survey responses but no one willing to pay.

When signals conflict, follow this decision tree:

1. **Identify the highest-tier signal.** If you have Tier 5 evidence (actual payment), trust it over Tier 1-3 signals (likes, views, interest). If you have Tier 4 evidence (booked calls, specific signups), trust it over Tier 2-3 (search volume, landing page visits).

2. **Quantify the uncertainty.** If 100 people signed up for a waitlist but only 2 converted to pre-order, your conversion rate is 2%. If your margin requires 10% conversion to break even, you have a 5× gap. Do not manufacture until you close that gap.

3. **Test the objection directly.** If people click but do not buy, test price. Run a second landing page with 20% lower price and measure conversion lift. If people sign up but do not pay, email them with a direct ask: "We are finalizing the first production run. Can we count you in for $X?"

4. **Separate seasonal and structural demand.** If search volume spiked in December but dropped 80% in January, the demand may be seasonal. Check 12-month trends. If demand is stable, the spike may be noise. If demand is seasonal, model inventory turns and storage cost for off-season months.

5. **Check for substitute products.** If people search but do not buy, they may already own a substitute. Search for "alternative to [your product]" and check reviews. If the top substitute has 4.5+ stars and 1,000+ reviews, your product needs a clear differentiator.

## Step-by-step validation workflow before placing your first order

Here is the complete workflow, from idea to manufacturing decision.

### Week 1: Demand hypothesis

1. Define the problem you are solving in one sentence.
2. Identify the target customer (demographics, budget, current substitute).
3. Search for the problem in the customer's own language (Reddit, TikTok, Amazon reviews, Google).
4. Collect 10+ specific complaints or feature requests.
5. Check 180-day search trend (Google Trends, TikTok search, Amazon autocomplete).

### Week 2: Landing page test

1. Build a landing page with product image, price, benefit bullet points, and CTA (waitlist or pre-order).
2. Drive 100-500 clicks from paid ads (Facebook, Google, TikTok) targeting exact customer.
3. Measure bounce rate, time on page, click-to-signup rate, click-to-purchase rate.
4. Collect objections from ad comments, landing page heatmaps, and exit surveys.

### Week 3: Margin and supply research

1. Request quotes from 3+ suppliers (Alibaba, domestic manufacturers, dropship partners).
2. Calculate landed cost per unit (product + shipping + duties + packaging).
3. Add platform fees, CAC, returns reserve, and support cost.
4. Model margin at 100, 500, 1,000 unit tiers.
5. Order samples from top 2 suppliers.

### Week 4: Operations planning

1. Inspect samples for quality, branding, packaging, and shipping time.
2. Choose fulfillment method (FBA, 3PL, self-fulfillment).
3. Map return and refund workflow.
4. Check compliance requirements (FDA, FTC, CPSC, platform restricted categories).
5. Create reorder plan (lead time, MOQ, payment terms).

### Week 5: Go/no-go decision

1. Review evidence across all four validation domains (demand, margin, supply, operations).
2. Identify the weakest link. If margin is under 20%, do not manufacture. If conversion is under 1%, do not manufacture. If supplier requires 100% payment upfront, do not manufacture.
3. If all four domains pass, place first order at minimum viable quantity (100-300 units for most categories).
4. If any domain fails, return to the riskiest assumption and run a cheaper test.

## Common validation mistakes and how to avoid them

**Mistake 1: Trusting AI-generated recommendations without external evidence.**

AI tools can generate product ideas, research summaries, and demand scores quickly. But an AI-generated product recommendation is only a hypothesis until checked against external evidence for live demand, competitor activity, supplier feasibility, and unit economics. The emerging buyer question is: "Can I audit the recommendation?" and "What data would make the AI change its mind?" Recent guidance recommends checking AI suggestions against Google Trends, TikTok Shop sales, Meta Ad Library activity, supplier quotes, shipping, duties, fees, returns, and customer-acquisition cost before funding a test.

**Mistake 2: Treating search volume as proof of demand.**

Search volume tells you how many people searched. It does not tell you whether they bought, what they paid, or whether anyone can manufacture and ship it profitably. High search volume often correlates with high competition, low margin, or saturated markets. Validate conversion and margin before manufacturing.

**Mistake 3: Skipping margin modeling until after the first order.**

Many sellers calculate margin for the first time after inventory arrives and they see the actual landed cost, platform fees, and return rate. By then, they have already committed $5,000 to $20,000. Model margin at the quote stage, before placing the order. If margin is under 20% at 1,000-unit scale, do not manufacture.

**Mistake 4: Accepting vanity metrics (likes, views, survey interest) as validation.**

Likes, views, and survey responses cost the user nothing. A person who clicks "interested" may never visit your landing page. A person who completes a survey may have no budget. A person who likes a TikTok may already own a substitute. Validate with Tier 4-5 evidence (booked calls, pre-orders, deposits, purchases).

**Mistake 5: Ignoring seasonality and trend decay.**

If search volume spiked in December but dropped 80% in January, the demand may be seasonal. If a TikTok trend generated 10,000 views in week 1 but 500 views in week 4, the trend is decaying. Check 180-day trends before manufacturing. Model inventory turns and storage cost for off-season months.

**Mistake 6: Manufacturing without testing operations.**

Many sellers focus on demand and margin but skip fulfillment, returns, customer service, and compliance. Then the product launches, return rate hits 25%, customer service takes 10 hours per 100 orders, and margin collapses. Map the operational workflow before placing the first order. Estimate return and support volume. Check compliance requirements.

## What to do when validation fails

Validation failure is not product failure. It is cheap information.

If demand validation fails (under 1% conversion after 500 landing page visits), you learned that the problem is not urgent enough, the price is too high, or the messaging does not connect. Do not manufacture. Return to the problem definition. Interview 5-10 people who clicked but did not sign up. Identify the real objection.

If margin validation fails (under 20% margin at 1,000-unit scale), you learned that the product cannot be sold profitably at a price customers will pay. Do not manufacture. Negotiate with the supplier for lower MOQ or better terms. Find a domestic supplier with faster shipping and lower duties. Simplify the product to reduce cost. Or pivot to a different product.

If supply validation fails (no supplier can produce the exact variant on workable terms), you learned that the product is not manufacturable at your scale. Do not manufacture. Simplify the design. Partner with a domestic manufacturer. Or pivot to a different product.

If operations validation fails (fulfillment cost exceeds 15% of sale price, or return rate is projected at 30%+), you learned that the product is operationally expensive. Do not manufacture. Switch to FBA or 3PL to reduce fulfillment cost. Improve product quality or packaging to reduce returns. Or pivot to a different product.

The goal of validation is to fail fast and cheap, not to convince yourself to manufacture. The cheapest failure is at the landing page stage ($500 in ad spend). The most expensive failure is after 1,000 units arrive and you discover 30% return rate, negative margin, and zero repeat purchase rate ($10,000 to $50,000 loss).
